* * *

When you grow up outside of Philadelphia, taking the train into the city feels as if it’s a holy pilgrimage. I used to stand on the platform, counting and re-counting the cash in my hand, making sure I had enough to buy a ticket. Some kids were allowed to take the train more casually — they had more money to spare, their parents monitored them less, they had more confidence in themselves — the list felt endless, really. Could they feel my envy from the opposite platform?

I used to eavesdrop on my classmates whenever they’d talk about taking the train. It would come up most during two major events: Eagles games, and the Made in America Festival. Oh, do I remember living through each of them vicariously. If I close my eyes, I can still feel myself crammed like sweaty sardines into the cars. A belligerent frat bro spills some of his beer on me. He smells of hoppy testosterone and stale Old Spice, and I pretend I’m not attracted to it. E-A-G-L-E-S — EAGLES! A high schooler greens out on the floor, babbling incoherently about Playboi Carti. He comes to drenched in sweat. I’m sure he wished he hadn’t “wokeuplikethis*”.

* * *

It wasn’t until my late adolescence that I had a fattening wallet and freedom. Detached from my fantasy, public transportation endangered me as much as it liberated me. One July morning, I caught a bus with my friend into Upper Darby. It would be the first time I met her boyfriend. It was positively sweltering and sticky-humid — the type of heat that brings every emotion to a rolling boil. Even the brief walk from 69th Street left me sunburned and dripping with sweat. Sitting in a lawn chair circle in his family’s unfinished rowhome basement, her boyfriend finally came home from work. He wasn’t happy to meet me.

Sometime between screaming at me, and screaming at her, he wrapped his hands around her throat as she sat on one of the chairs. Second after second passed as he throttled her, and I sat frozen as I watched her turn deeper shades of purple. When I finally gathered the courage to intervene — out of fear that, if I didn’t, her death would haunt my conscience for eternity — he pulled a pistol from the drawer of a filing cabinet. He had a smooth and steady aim. I still remember staring into the black hole of the gun’s barrel. Nietzsche was correct: the abyss does stare back. I wouldn’t understand that for a while.

My friend — gasping and sputtering, with drool hanging from her lips — stepped between us, and was miraculously able to calm him down. He seemed to be largely pacified and kind the rest of the afternoon. Later, I would come to understand that as the cycle of abuse: the honeymoon phase after the explosion. He bought me a thing of cherry water ice at Rita’s — my favorite flavor. He used his ID to buy me a new bong from the smoke shop. I held it as if it were some cross between a newborn baby and a bat — a one-trick shattering pony that could buy me a head-start.

As we sat back in the basement, he paid particular attention to me. He was funny and charming at times, even. If I willed myself to forget what he had just done, I could almost understand why my friend fell for him. The gun never left his lap. He’d aim his gun at me in between each joke — sometimes, he’d aim it as the joke. It was as if I was being held hostage at a killer clown’s comedy club. He’d giggle as I cowered and pleaded with him to stop aiming it at me. Sometimes, he’d just say: “bang”. More guffaws. Huh-huh. I felt like every “bang” was a jump to a new universe. I think I died in fifty lifetimes in that basement.

A little before he released me, he let me buy weed off of his uncle. My eighth turned into a quarter on the house — lucky me. He paid for my Uber back home. I’ve never felt so relieved to be back in a car. My driver was an unassuming man — Pakistani, he explained to me. We spent an hour in bumper-to-bumper rush hour traffic on the Blue Route, and I waited with bated breath for him to lose his temper. He never did.

I spent the rest of the night loading bowl after bowl in my new bong, smoking until the trembling in my hands finally stopped. I broke the bong a few days later, trying to shove an ice cube into the stem. I guess that serves me right.

Years later, my friend would update me that he’s in prison now for first-degree murder. It’s for life, with no chance of parole.

* * *

A couple months later, during my senior year of high school, I was asked out for the very first time. He was a quiet guy — unassuming and preppy-looking. We had spent weeks eyeing each other in the parking lot before school. He drove a VW Bug, although the color is something I’ve forgotten. If it weren’t for the beard and deep voice, I would have thought that he was too feminine to take seriously. But he came with an offering of Mac DeMarco tickets at The Mann, and it was an offer I couldn’t refuse. Perhaps he was more masculine than I thought, presenting an expensive gift at my perch. I accepted it without much of a second thought, and he picked me up that hot September afternoon.

The illusion began to break down at a gas station in West Philly, where we had stopped for rolling papers. A strange man accosted us, and he actually stopped to speak to him. My date looked like he couldn’t throw a punch to begin with. And now, as I roasted in the reflection of the store’s metal sheeting, he had actually given this man attention. Isn’t it Street Smarts 101 to ignore strangers hollering at you at a gas station? I couldn’t tell if my face burned with fear and cringe, or from the heat of the sun and sheeting. Either way, I was an ant under a magnifying glass.

I should have known what was coming next when he said at the concert, “I feel like that weed was laced.” No, it wasn’t. I was perfectly fine. You, my friend, simply lied about being a man with a drug tolerance. He disappeared sometime during “Salad Days”. I didn’t have much time to wonder where he’d gone before I heard some man screaming: “Get out the way! Get out the way! Move!”

They were carrying his unconscious body to the medical tent.

My first instinct was to flush the weed I had left, lest I should find out he was the type to rat. I watched the buds spiral and swirl in the metal shitter, disappearing into the abyss alongside my time and dignity. Maybe I was a bad person for not coming to see him. But I figured, he didn’t know that I knew where he went — and he was in good hands with the paramedics. Well, I didn’t want him ratting on me, either. With a dying phone, I dug out some dollar bills I had stuffed into my shoe before I left the house.

Sitting and waiting for my Uber under the twilight sky, I heard Mac DeMarco playing “My Kind of Woman”. In that moment, I felt like no one’s kind of woman. I felt like I would never be anyone’s kind of woman. I caught the train home from Overbrook, letting it hold me in its sturdy grasp as I gazed out the window.

* * *

By the time we graduated high school, my friend had found a new boyfriend. They lived in North Philly, in some dingy apartment over a meth lab. The golden rule was to always hold your breath in the hallways. The walls were yellow from paint, and cigarette smoke, and whatever ungodly emissions come from meth manufacturing. It felt romantic walking into her apartment. Their futon could hold four people — easy — and a gigantic ash tray on the coffee table could hold endless smoked-out blunts. There were no suburbanites calling the police on us for loitering anymore. The papi store at the end of the block dished out food all night, slipping it out underneath the metal bars and bulletproof glass. I’d limit myself to a handful of French fries, despite the cheesesteaks looking and smelling tantalizing. To be a woman was to be thin and dainty.

I’d keep my mouth busy the rest of the night with mango Juul pods, and conversations with her boyfriend about philosophy. It leaves a lot to chew on. She would sleep between us on the futon, passed out from getting crossfaded. I’ll never admit to anyone that I found myself attracted to him. Thoughts can’t hurt others if they don’t turn into actions. Once, when I was blackout drunk, I slipped my hand into his shirt pocket on a balcony. I stole a cigarette from him, giggling the entire time. I demanded he light it for me, and then I put it out on his arm by accident. He never hurt me back. That was the year we fell in love with each other.

One day, my friend left her license at my Chinatown friend’s apartment. I drove with the Chinatown friend to return it. I loved her apartment — it had the coldest AC I had ever felt in my life. You had to use the bathroom in the business downstairs. In the eyes of the City of Philadelphia, it was an “illegal dwelling”. For her, it was sanctuary. I learned from her how to bat my eyes at a man when I need something from him. I had watched her grow up since middle school, blossoming from a nerd into a bottle girl. Sometimes, I couldn’t reconcile the two in my head. Sometimes, I wondered when I would finally arrive where she had. I always seemed to fall short — although, looking back, the timing was out of my control.

She told me to stay in the car while she walked the ID up. A gaggle of guys sat on the neighboring stoop. I stared out the windshield as they gawked at her ass. Some of them whistled. Some of them whooped at her. Somehow, she ignored them without missing a beat. Behind the windshield, I wielded a cell phone in one hand and a baseball bat in the other — the backseat bat, our dear friend and skull-cracker. When she got back in the car, she didn’t even comment on the guys on the stoop. I suppose that, for her, she had grown numb to it. From sugar daddies to nightclub sleazeballs, she had thick skin. I didn’t know where my admiration ended and my envy began. I remember sharing a joint with her as Khalid’s “OTW” blasted on the car radio. She seemed so confident that the police wouldn’t pull her over — and that if they did, she would be able to get out of it. I believed her.

* * *

My Chinatown friend’s place quickly became one of my favorite summer refuges. We’d step out onto the streets, weaving through the rows and clusters of street stalls. We’d giggle with each other, wondering how much weed we just stole from her boyfriend. My mind would spin with the shapes and colors of the produce, jewelry, and garments. It was kaleidoscopic. There’s a way that gasoline mixes with the smell of summer concrete, concentrating in every street like engine cylinders. I felt propelled with every step. I would almost trip on my own feet, too distracted by reading Chinese signs and dodging crowds of people.

Walking into her favorite restaurant, you would be immediately greeted with a glass case of roasted ducks. At first glance, I couldn’t make out their body parts. I suppose that, if I had really focused, I could have eventually made them out. I didn’t take the time. It was something that, at the time, I didn’t want to have to fully reflect upon. Eating duck is fun when you start with the plate, not the slaughterhouse lineup. I sat with my back to the case, giggling with my friend over hot tea. With her recommendation, we both ordered the duck off the menu. It was good, I suppose — the flesh was tender. It contained whatever combination of macro and micronutrients we need to sustain ourselves. I was probably too stoned to offer an honest, unbiased tasting opinion. After our meal, I caught a train home from Jefferson.

Shortly thereafter, my stomach became a war zone. After weeks of severe gastrointestinal illness, I received a contact tracing call from the health department. I had contracted a disease that, according to the tracer, was “usually only found in Chinese immigrant children under the age of 6”. To this day, I quip that I should have been patient zero for COVID-19 in America.

* * *

By the time I started medically transitioning in my late teens, I think I had made peace with what I was getting myself into — as much peace as one can make with it. How do you make peace as you walk towards the guillotine, or the hanging tree? Regality, dissociation, drugs — pick your anesthetic. It started with a glance here, a glance there. Glances turned to stares. Stares fragmented into epithets, and giggles, and more intense stares. My North Philly friend’s boyfriend had spent years devolving into a serial cheater. Within months, I noticed him starting to stare at me longer. My friend had once told me, “You’re my only friend that he doesn’t have a problem with.” I found that to be true. Sometimes, I felt as if I was catching fire from his gaze. Somewhere in between guilt and shame still lived desire, like dry logs on a bonfire. They all created heat with a compounding effect. I never knew if I should have mentioned anything to my friend. Would she hate me? Would she become defensive, not wanting to believe her boyfriend liked trans women too? Would she be even more traumatized by yet another episode of wandering? Was it all in my head? I kept it to myself.

His stares split into more intense stares, and then touches. It started with him sitting behind me on the train, and evolved to him walking by my side to the bars, leaving my friend to walk behind us. His shoulder would brush up against me sometimes. It felt warm and sturdy. I had to stop here and there to fix it, to call my friend forward — and time and time again, he would fall back to my side. Regardless, he would train his focus back on me. My friend became background noise to him, really. Every question was custom-tailored to me, and me only. No man bothered us when he was there, though. I suppose that was nice.

Soon, his hand would find my back when we were weaving through crowds. Do men guide their friends through crowds with a hand on their lower back? I wasn’t sure — or maybe I was in denial. Then, he would start buying me drinks — White Claws, vodka sodas, whatever my thirsty heart desired. He told me I owed him the next round. I told him, coyly, that I don’t buy drinks for men. I heard my Chinatown friend in my voice. I think that’s the moment that I became complicit, in a way.

One night, months later, I found myself with them again at a nightclub. While I was briefly separated from them, a strange man put his hands on my hips, grazing past me as he walked by. His face dropped when he turned to look at me. Moments later, he shoulder-checked me when he walked by again. I spilled my drink all over myself and the floor. He never apologized or stopped.

Later, still half-wet with vodka, my friend’s boyfriend was behind the both of us. He asked us why we weren’t dancing, encouraging us to dance with him. He put his left hand on her shoulder. He put his right hand on my shoulder, before he dragged it down my back, rubbing and massaging it all along the way to my tailbone. I felt myself freeze.

I felt the duck flap her wings in my stomach.

* * *

Gwenyth Galloway is a poet and writer native to Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Her work explores topics including environmentalism, interpersonal relationships, and the rise of contemporary American fascism.
