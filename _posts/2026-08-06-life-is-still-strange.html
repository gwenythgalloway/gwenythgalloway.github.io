I used to fetishize the aesthetic of smoking while sad. Like every other 14-year-old anorexic-wannabe queer on Tumblr, I saw myself as the lovechild of Skins’ Effy Stonem and Cassie Ainsworth: a food-starved, acid-dropping rebel. Give me a cigarette, and stick me in a black-and-white GIF. I didn’t eat for three days so I could be lovely — and fuck strange DILFs off Grindr in their garages, all while blitzed on molly. I don’t think that was supposed to be in the script. Lovely.

It becomes less glamorous when you’re nearing 30. Suddenly, your biggest concerns are all different: tretinoin from Vanuatu (you’re a wrinkly hag), finishing your degree (you’ll definitely die destitute), and finding a husband (you’ll die alone, too). My knees hurt if I don’t do my morning floor stretches. I have a penchant for soluble fiber and peach kombucha (microbiomemaxxing). When I feel particularly fancy, I make homemade rejuvelac. Kombucha is for posers — fermented quinoa water is for true probiotic lovers. Suddenly, I can no longer justify spending $60 a week on Juul pods. Oh, wow.

As such, I can mostly manage my nostalgia for that side of smoking. What I can’t manage is my longing for the Life Is Strange cigarette: smoked in my best friend’s bed, with the morning light streaming in through an American-flag-turned-curtain. The garage DILF is now a bad nightmare, as he always should have been. There’s no longer any pressure to perform, or to be consumed, or to be seen as consumable — pretty, hot, fuckable. Our Polaroid selfies are strung up behind us. I see the light streaming in on her sleeping face, and I swallow down my agonizing biromantic longing.

It’s easy to extrapolate some grand love story with some minor changes. Her room transforms to have some more sensible decor now. She reads the Bible every night before bed — not because she’s reverting to Catholicism, but because it’s important to be well-read. I can smell her Jergens hand lotion. I still wake up next to her in bed. There’s no putrid, demanding, testosterone-ridden ogre snoring next to me — or, even worse, an empty spot where my mattress looks brand new — it never had the chance to develop a dip.

Maybe I’m being too harsh on men. Or, maybe, I’m not being harsh enough. Maybe I’m scarred from being dragged through the mud by them. I’ve deeply, desperately loved men throughout my life — but doing so has brought me more trauma than satisfaction. I suspect it’s due to the compounding effect of misogyny on natural differentiation. Love with women has always been so simple for me: there’s mutual understanding and a shared language. That’s how I felt with the last woman I loved. The last paragraph was, transparently, about her. One night, in the dying breaths of our Skins eras, she spent an entire night pulling trig for me (translation for boomers: she spent an entire night shoving her hand down my throat because I was too drunk, and I was too scared to make myself throw up). I can still feel her arm wrapped around my near-blacked-out, toilet-hugging body.

You’d sooner find a man who would do this for his mental porn stash than a man who’d do this out of love. She’s engaged to a man who I was never quite a fan of — deep-seated jealousy aside. We haven’t spoken in years now. I wish I would have told her I loved her. Deep down, I know I never would have — and deep down, I know we were nothing more than friends. I was too feminine, even when I was still a boy. I never could have been the man she needed. And most importantly, I could never be monogamously married to a woman. If I could have married a woman and been “normal”, though, it would have been her.

I used to eat turkey burgers without the bun while playing Life Is Strange. How else would I hit my Ultimate Goal Weight™ on myproana (the most popular old-school “pro-anorexia” forum, for those out of the loop)? It’s “ED Support Forum” now, apparently. Times really have changed. I feel as if the world has moved on without me: where the demands of the male gaze have remained just as excruciating, while all the fun ways of living up to it aren’t acceptable anymore. Or feasible. Or, specifically, feasible for someone of my advanced age (late-20s are the new 90s, apparently. My joints believe them.)

I’ve spent so much time trying to figure all of it out: how can I best be beautiful to men, if they’re the only ones I’m compatible with long-term? Should I just give up and “decenter” them? Were they always a fairy tale? Am I doomed to being trapped in nonreciprocal love with women? It’s not the fault of women, nor is it mine. I love the male form and being — the shoulders, the chests, the body hair, the jaws, the beards, the voices — I like some aspects of male socialization, as well. I like traditional masculinity. It’s yin-and-yang. Oh, the woes of an involuntarily heterosexual woman. It’s a problem that’s seemingly unique to heterosexual women: an unwilling love of those they’re attracted to. Lesbian women enthusiastically love women. Straight men enthusiastically love women. Gay men enthusiastically love men. It’s straight women who look at men and go, “Ugh, fine. I guess.”

Really, I never imagined how much Max Caulfield would continue to resonate with my life. I’ve found my life playing out the same way I played Life Is Strange: I want to kiss Warren, I want to be with Warren, yet life inevitably pushes me to be with Chloe. I really, truly wish I could be with Chloe. I will kiss her before I teleport back in time, sliding down the bathroom stall as I’m forced to allow my classmate to murder her. I will have simultaneously loved her, and been incapable of loving her. I will always sacrifice her to save the greater number of lives. And I hope that, when the credits finally roll, it will be Warren by my side.

Gwenyth Galloway is a poet and writer native to Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Her work explores topics including environmentalism, interpersonal relationships, and the rise of contemporary American fascism.
