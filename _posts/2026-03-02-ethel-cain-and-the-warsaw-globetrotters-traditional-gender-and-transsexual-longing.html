♫ Put away basketball / go to work / in calcium mine ♫

I often wonder how much my perception of cisgender, heterosexual female desire has been determined by the circumstances of my existence: an unmistakably queer "f*ggot" turned transsexual. Are the women most likely to have me as a friend also the most likely to desire a "progressive" man? In any case, my interpersonal friendships and social media trawling have presented a common theme: a man who is, by all accounts, "modern" in his behavior - "metrosexual", as some call it. He uses a body wash that's meant purely for the body. He cleanses his hair with a shampoo that doesn't come with conditioner already in it - let alone, god forbid, a 3-in-1 that lacks conditioner entirely. He has a skincare routine. He has a favorite moisturizer and sunscreen. He doesn't believe that either are a threat to his testicular volume or testosterone production. He probably even says that physical touch is his preferred "love language".

"Babe, my therapist told me that you not sucking my dick daily is preventing me from developing secure attachment. By the way, do you have your half of the rent money?”

One of the first times I ever witnessed my “type” in media was with the “Warsaw Globetrotters”, a humorous parody on Family Guy of the famous Harlem Globetrotters basketball team. The premise is simple: three nonchalant Polish men, with stubble rough enough to sand wood to a silky grain, dressed in stained garments filthy with oil and grease, cut their performance short to return to the calcium mine. They have chest hair poking out of their tank tops, and functional guts holding some combination of muscle and visceral vodka fat. A homely woman looks on from the stands, donned in an apron and with a scarf on her head, looking ready for the backbreaking toils of the factory or farm. The gym around them is crumbling, covered in grime and dirt, with bricks falling out of the walls.

It’s the aesthetic that has come to stereotype the former Soviet bloc : an aesthetic reminiscent of one’s ancestors from a genealogy deep-dive — of one’s great-great-grandmother who birthed 14 children over the course of her lifetime. It’s an aesthetic borne of abject working-class poverty, of hard industrial and agricultural labor, and of the political right’s wet dream of “RETVRNing” to tradition. It is, in essence, an aesthetic of raw material, of earnest attempts at maximizing potential, and of a firm divide between the social, political, and economic spheres of women and men. Why, then, would a modern transsexual woman find herself longing for a man who evokes an era that is seemingly incompatible with her existence?

My friends alternate between some combination of chastisement, bewilderment, and genuine concern for my well-being. The theme has been one and the same since my youth — from my early days of being told that “Team Edward” was vastly superior to “Team Jacob”, to my teenage years of my friends continuing to fangirl over vampiric twinks while I prayed for a Neanderthalic lumberjack to come to life, drag me to his (self-constructed) cabin in the woods, and allow me to tend to my dream garden of garlic and herbs. The insinuation has been clear: that I’m “regressive”, wrestling with “internalized misogyny and femphobia”, and sabotaging my prospects of having a fulfilling relationship. How is a caveman supposed to be emotionally fulfilling and intelligent? How am I supposed to be happy with a man who communicates in grunts and grumbles?

To my friends: I offer you a one-up and a diversion. A viral social media post by transsexual music star Ethel Cain (pictured above), posted in reference to her 2025 album Perverts, details her sexual longing for the Bruce Mansfield Power Plant in Shippingport, PA. At least a caveman —a Homo sapiens —is human under the popular definition. At least a caveman can communicate in some capacity, even if it predates advanced linguistic communication. Yet, as humorous as I find it to be upon first glance, the more I sit with it, the more I realize that it isn't "extreme" or alien in comparison to my tastes - it's my own taste embodied in something ostensibly inhuman. The power plant speaks in its own form of grunts and grumbles: it speaks in pulses of energy and steam. Much like the Polish miner, it reads to me as (hyper)masculinity, as "traditional masculinity", and as power —literal power, in this instance. And for Ethel Cain, a straight transsexual woman with an alleged affinity for Marlboro-smelling roughnecks (half-looking at you, Jack Donoghue), I don't believe that's coincidental. But why does the power plant read as "traditionally masculine"?

"He looks like he works with his hands / and smells like Marlboro Reds / it makes me so, uh, and I can't get enough of it" - Crush by Ethel Cain

I see "traditional masculinity", and hence hypermasculinity, as a performance of strength and functionality — notably exceptional physical performance and stature, combined with a sort of emotional detachment or vacancy. Stripped to its core, I see little difference between its aesthetic and the precise aesthetic of the power plant: towering, emotionless, and sturdy. It's the type of man who can survive a 16-hour shift in the mine, sustained by nothing but liquor and a fat hunk of hearty rye bread. The power plant embodiment almost magnifies it: the mine's extractions poured into man's creation, returning a massively compounded investment on its own labor. If laboring in the mine is masculine, the imposing power plant is hypermasculine. A "giant concrete boyfriend", indeed. In publicly admitting attraction to the most quintessential brutalist brute imaginable —giant, concrete cooling towers — Ethel brought an exaggerated, almost popularly grotesque depiction of hypermasculine attraction to the public sphere. What's left for me when the warm feeling of mutual recognition fades is a curiosity: why do I know of so many straight transsexual women who liked hypermasculinity, and why do I find myself amongst them?

Of course, human sexuality and eroticism is not universal nor cleanly attributed to any particular group. It would be foolish of me to state otherwise. However, in my personal experience, I’ve found that there is a marked prevalence of an attraction to hypermasculinity amongst heterosexual transsexual women. Unfortunately, attempting to discuss any perceived “trend” of an attraction (or erotic trigger) in transsexual women doesn’t come without controversy — thanks to the largely discredited and highly controversial academic work by sexologist Ray Blanchard, credited for the creation of a typology of transsexual desire split mainly into two categories: “autogynophilic transsexuals” and “homosexual transsexuals”. Under his typology, the two groups can crudely be described as such: the former are naturally masculine female-attracted “men” who are aroused by the thought of their own femininity, and the latter are naturally feminine male-attracted “men” who are attracted to heterosexual men. The former is stereotyped for transitioning late and being unable to pass as the opposite sex; whereas the latter is stereotyped for transitioning young and having success with passing as the opposite sex. As such, both myself and Ethel Cain would be described as “homosexual” transsexuals: feminine, young transitioners who are attracted to the ostensibly heterosexual “natal sex”. But what does “ostensibly” heterosexual mean within the popular context? What is traditional heterosexual male performance if anything other than (hyper)masculinity? And why, exactly, can (hyper)masculinity — and, hence, the power plant — be erotic for a transsexual woman?

If I wanted to engage in a surface-level analysis, I’d simply posit that it’s entirely natural for a heterosexual woman to be attracted to the stereotypical, socially-conditioned and expected cues of a heterosexual man. Case closed. But in an era where the range of socially permissible “masculine” behaviors has expanded, why would one find themselves attracted to the older, brutish version? By the time a transsexual woman has reached adulthood, she has likely experienced no shortage of oppression and denigration. Often stripped of social understanding, family support, and the dignity of generating income from a source other than sex work, the brutalist power plant almost becomes not only the yin to the yang; but the promise of bountiful creation, stability, and security from a plentiful labor that is suited to sustaining the lives around it. It is the security of a warm bedroom, a safe container, and a protection from a nature that seems dead-set on predating upon it. This, of course, is not to say that women under “traditional femininity” do not perform labor (nor that their labor isn’t essential to creating material safety) — but, rather, that their labor is predicated upon attaining the raw material (ie. social and financial capital) that has been withheld from them under a patriarchal system. As written by Anne Morrow Lindbergh in her book Gift from the Sea,

"The life I have chosen as a wife and mother entrains a whole host of complications. It involves a house in the suburbs and either household drudgery or household help which wavers between scarcity and non-existence for most of us. It involves food and shelter; meals, planning, marketing, bills … it involves not only the butcher, the baker, the candlestickmaker but countless other experts to keep my modern house with its modern “simplifications” … functioning properly … it involves education, spiritual, intellectual, physical; schools, school conferences, car-pools, extra trips for basketball or orchestra practice … it involves clothes, laundry, shopping, cleaning … my mind reels with it. What a circus act we women perform every day of our lives. It puts the trapeze artist to shame.”

“Traditional womanhood” is not devoid of labor at all — it is chock-full of endless, invisible, nearly boundless labor that comes with little social thanks or recognition. It offers no paycheck that doesn’t first pass through the husband. But in essence, it is supposed to be a promise from the male partner: “I’ll do the job of bringing the raw materials. It’s your job to turn it into a household and family.” In modern Western countries, a cisgender woman is now ostensibly able to freely access the job market (albeit with a reduced earning per dollar compared to cisgender men) and has the ability to gather the raw materials needed to survive either independently or with a family unit — so much so that, to many cisgender women, male partners are now something to be decentered: something that is no longer necessary to survival. There is no longer a necessity for them to swallow the trauma that so many women experience in interacting with men: a lack of emotional connection and nourishment, domestic exploitation/subjugation/violence, and providing invisible and thankless labor to the men in their lives.

But where does that leave both vulnerable transsexual women (especially visibly and legally queer transsexual women), and cisgender women in locations where they’re unable to access the labor market to the same degree? A popular theory of why a woman may choose a more “rugged” or “traditional” man lies in the idea that, in unstable and dangerous times, a male partner’s physical and financial resources matter more than their emotional landscape. Emotional connection becomes secondary to safety needs, because it’s impossible to focus on emotional connection while starving and freezing. Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, while criticized for being culturally biased against non-Western countries, does still seem to have some credence here.

Under this framework, I believe that the desire of a hypermasculine partner becomes sort of all-encompassing fix or salve to the transsexual woman: the promise of physiological needs being met through the provision of raw resources, the ostensible safety of being considered worth protecting, the promise of love and belonging, and the ability to (attempt to) cultivate self-esteem and self-actualization during their prison sentence. For Ethel, and for other transsexual women, I imagine that the brutalism and functionality of hypermasculinity truly can seem like the path to self-actualization — as Ethel describes it in her post, a “beacon of religiosity, of sexual liberation and enjoyment, of contentedness.”

But through what avenues can one go about obtaining raw material resources from hypermasculine men? Under the traditional framework, there are two options: marriage and sex work. As illustrated by Andrea Dworkin in her novel Right Wing Women,

"Right-wing women have surveyed the world: they find it a dangerous place. They see that work subjects them to more danger from more men; it increases the risk of sexual exploitation. They see that creativity and originality in their kind are ridiculed; they see women thrown out of the circle of male civilization for having ideas, plans, visions, ambitions. They see that traditional marriage means selling to one man, not hundreds: the better deal. They see that the streets are cold, and that the women on them are tired, sick, and bruised. They see that the money they can earn will not make them independent of men and that they will still have to play the sex games of their kind: at home and at work too. They see no way to make their bodies authentically their own and to survive in the world of men. They know too that the Left has nothing better to offer: leftist men also want wives and whores; leftist men value whores too much and wives too little. Right-wing women are not wrong … Right-wing women see that within the system in which they live they cannot make their bodies their own, but they can agree to privatized male ownership: keep it one-on-one, as it were. They know that they are valued for their sex — their sex organs and their reproductive capacity … They use sex and babies to stay valuable because they need a home, food, clothing. They use the traditional intelligence of the female — animal, not human: they do what they have to to survive.”

Venus Xtravaganza, the transsexual ballroom belle immortalized by the 1990 movie Paris is Burning, echoes this sentiment:

“Later on this evening, I’m supposed to meet someone … a young, very good, attractive, handsome young man … he’s taking me out to dinner later on this evening, or for cocktails after midnight. I know he’ll give me some money, just for me to maybe buy a pair of shoes and a nice dress, so that the next time he sees me, he’ll see me looking more and more beautiful — the way he wants to see me.”

“If you’re married — a woman, in the suburbs, a regular woman — is married to her husband, and she wants him to buy her a washer and dryer set — in order for him to buy that, I’m sure she’d have to go to bed with him anyway to give him what he wants, for her to get what she wants. So, in the long run, it all ends up the same way.”

“I want a car. I want to be with the man I love. I want a nice home — away from New York — up the Peekskills, or maybe in Florida — somewhere far, where no one knows me. I want my sex change. I want to get married in church in white. I want to be a complete woman.”

During the filming of Paris is Burning, Venus was found under a mattress at the Fulton Hotel in New York City, strangled to death and left to decompose by her murderer (presumably a john). Despite correctly identifying that the fundamental economic exchange in both marriage and survival sex were identical, she nonetheless fantasized about being private property under her own framework — beloved property, yes, but property nonetheless. When she discusses how one client takes her to dinner and gives her money to purchase clothing, so that she can be “more beautiful”, it is a manifestation of the behavior of the Polish miner: provide raw materials and reap the rewards. Except, in this case, it’s the miner’s stop at the brothel before he returns home to his wife, paycheck and lunch pail in tow. It is the “traditional masculinity” framework watered down into its crudest and rawest form within the context of the sex industry.

I believe that her desire for a church wedding specifically is also telling: it signifies acceptance and belonging within the dominant conservative, religious institution that marginalized her and other LGBTQ individuals. Venus, born into a Catholic family (and now buried in a Catholic cemetery), craved inclusion within the system that had driven her from her birth family. As someone who was likewise born into a Catholic family, I’ve found myself in parallel fantasies. Poland, an overwhelmingly Catholic and conservative country, finds itself with one of the largest number of churches per capita in the entire world. The Warsaw Globetrotter man is the convergence of it all: the raw resource, the material protection, the community acceptance; all packaged neatly into a singular man. I believe that it is, in essence, (at least in part) a misguided desire to escape oppression, while swimming into the open mouth of a shark with orthodontic work. It’s a little prettier and more socially acceptable, but the economic dynamic remains fundamentally unchanged.

Famous transsexual YouTube creator Kat Blaque, known for her nearly 2 decades of transsexual and racial advocacy, recently spoke on her thoughts surrounding romantic and sexual interaction with conservative men, stating, “I wouldn’t share my body with a man who wouldn’t politically defend it.” Born and raised in the interim between Paris is Burning and mainstream transsexual awareness/rights, she has previously described her experience catering her aesthetic in her formative years to the conservative male gaze, including being in a relationship with a man to whom she wished to be monogamously married. Likewise, she has spoken on several occasions about her experience being sexually assaulted and exploited by a particular client during her stint in survival sex work. And, perhaps coincidentally, she likewise has proudly expressed her attraction to hypermasculine men. Now financially stable through her creative work, while still having a taste for hypermasculine men, she is both openly polyamorous and an advocate for sex positivity and kink. And perhaps most importantly, she is not just openly polyamorous and sex-positive — she states that she is enthusiastically happy and emotionally satisfied with her partners. When digesting her content under this framework, I wonder two things: how much of her preference for hypermasculinity stemmed from those experiences, and how much of the ability to “indulge” in it in a safe manner came from gaining, through her creative work, the access to the raw financial resources that were previously denied and withheld from her?

As a transsexual woman in her mid-to-late 20s, I feel an eerie disconnect when I see younger transsexual women and girls online, many of whom transitioned in their early-to-mid teens (or even earlier). With an almost guaranteed ability to “pass”, a supportive family network, and legal recognition and support — I wonder if they’ll find themselves drawn to hypermasculinity at all. And, in my cursory foray into their experiences, I don’t personally see the same phenomenon amongst that generational cohort. I see, in essence, transsexual women who are indistinguishable in taste profile from my cisgender female friends. Is it from the ability to feel relatively physically safer and “blend” into society with a better ease? Is it from the fortune of having a relatively more secure upbringing? Is it from the relative lack of trauma in having to explicitly defend oneself against the onslaught of (and the attempted inclusion into) hypermasculine culture?

And as we collectively witness the legal and social onslaught against transsexual individuals (particularly transsexual youth), and the vigorous attempts at revitalizing traditional gender roles and hypermasculinity under the christofascist Trump regime, what will that spell for the next generation of transsexual women? As eerie as the feeling I experience is when I view younger transsexual women, my truest fear is in witnessing them be dragged back into the Dark Ages. Although it was isolating in feeling as if I was born just a little too early to live a more “normal” existence, it was comforting to feel as if I was of the last substantial crop of traumatized individuals. With ever-increasing restrictions on access to hormone replacement therapy, gender marker changes, and other gender-affirming care; as well as rollbacks on access to sports, bathrooms, and other facets of everyday life; it feels as if I’m standing on the precipice of falling back to my earliest years, and then through the floor down to my deceased transsexual ancestors.

So will I ever stop longing for the Warsaw Globetrotter men? Perhaps when I achieve more material safety, I’ll feel less pressured to choose a man who seems to embody all of my basest needs in a single form. Perhaps I should consciously remind myself that, even with the modern American transsexual’s (current) access to legally recognized marriage, that traditional marriage is nothing more than a prettier pair of teeth. But that opens another can of worms, doesn’t it? How much of human sexuality and taste is nature versus nurture? How can one change their erotic and romantic desires? And, on a primal and selfish level, how much time do I actually want to devote to this topic while tangled in smokey bedsheets next to some Polish miner? I’ve never found intellectualism to be particularly tender or erotic. But, I suppose in the meantime, I can just write into the ether.

Gwenyth Galloway is a poet and writer native to Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Her work explores topics including environmentalism, interpersonal relationships, and the rise of contemporary American fascism.
